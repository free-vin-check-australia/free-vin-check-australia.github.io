What is a VIN number?

A vehicle identification number, or VIN, is a 17-character code that identifies one specific vehicle. No two vehicles built in the same 30-year period share a VIN, which is why it works like a fingerprint for cars, motorcycles, trucks, trailers and caravans. The VIN appears on the vehicle itself, on the compliance plate and on registration, insurance and sale documents.

Modern 17-character VINs were standardised from 1981. In Australia, road vehicles supplied from 1 January 1989 must carry a 17-character VIN under Australian Design Rule 61. Older vehicles may have a shorter chassis number. For full detail read our guide to what a VIN number means.

How a VIN is structured

Every 17-character VIN is split into sections. The first three characters name the maker and region, the next five describe the vehicle, character 9 is a check digit, and the last eight identify the individual vehicle.

1Region / countryThe country or region of manufacture. For example 6 is Australia, J is Japan, K is South Korea, W is Germany and 1, 4 or 5 is the United States.
1-3World Manufacturer Identifier (WMI)Identifies the maker and vehicle type or division, such as 6G1 for Holden or JTD for Toyota.
4-8Vehicle Descriptor Section (VDS)Model, body style, engine and similar attributes. Each maker uses its own codes.
9Check digitA calculated value used to catch typing errors and altered numbers.
10Model yearA letter or number that represents the model year, repeating every 30 years.
11Plant codeThe factory where the vehicle was assembled.
12-17Serial numberThe production sequence number that makes the VIN unique.

The VIN check digit explained

The ninth character is a check digit under the ISO 3779 standard. Each character is converted to a number, multiplied by a fixed weight for its position, and the total is divided by 11. The remainder becomes the check digit, with 10 shown as X. If one character is mistyped, the digit will usually no longer match.

Do not panic about a mismatch. The check digit is only mandatory for North American vehicles. Many Australian, Japanese and European vehicles use position 9 differently, so a genuine VIN can fail. Use a mismatch as a reason to compare the VIN on the windscreen, compliance plate and paperwork, not as proof of fraud.

Model year and country codes

Position 10 holds the model year code. The letters I, O, Q, U and Z and the digit 0 are never used. Because the sequence repeats every 30 years, one code has two possible years. For example, A means 1980 or 2010, and 3 means 2003 or 2033. Use the build date on the compliance plate to confirm which one applies.

What the Check & Decode button shows

After the instant format check, the decode asks the NHTSA vPIC public database for any record it holds against the VIN. Where there is one, you see the make, model, model year, manufacturer, vehicle and body type, series and trim, engine details, fuel type, drive type, transmission and the plant location.

Limits of the decode. NHTSA vPIC is a United States catalogue. It does not include every Australian-market model, older vehicle or import, so you may see limited details or none. Always confirm the details against the vehicle, its compliance plate and its paperwork. A decode is not a physical identity check.

Need to check the Register of Approved Vehicles (RAV)?

The Register of Approved Vehicles is an Australian Government register of road vehicles supplied in Australia under the Road Vehicle Standards Act 2018. Its public search takes a VIN, which should match the number permanently marked on the vehicle. Use the RAV public search if you want to look for a matching entry. A RAV search is separate from a VIN decoder and does not replace a PPSR search.

Checks to do before buying a used car

  • Match the VIN on the windscreen, compliance plate and registration papers.
  • Run a PPSR search to find out if there is finance owing and whether the car is recorded as written off or stolen.
  • Check the registration status with the state road authority.
  • Ask for the service history and compare the odometer reading with the records.
  • Get an independent pre-purchase inspection. A mechanic can also spot signs a VIN or chassis number has been tampered with.
  • Check for open safety recalls, including the Takata airbag recall.

Common VIN problems

  • The VIN is hard to read. Photograph it in good light and compare it with the registration papers. If you still cannot read it, ask the seller to confirm it in writing.
  • The VIN contains I, O or Q. It was almost certainly misread. Try 1 for I and 0 for O.
  • The VINs do not match. If the windscreen, compliance plate and paperwork disagree, do not go ahead until the seller explains it.
  • The VIN is shorter than 17 characters. This is normal for older vehicles built before the 17-character standard. For those, the PPSR uses the chassis number.
  • The last three characters are not numbers. Australian Government guidance says the last three characters of a VIN must be numbers. Some older or foreign-market vehicles differ, so compare the VIN with the compliance plate and papers.
  • The year code looks wrong. The year in the VIN is the maker's model year. Compare it with the compliance plate build date.

Frequently asked questions

Is this VIN check really free?

Yes. The format check, check digit test and decode of country, maker and model year are free and have no sign-up. The official PPSR search is a separate government service that charges a fee.

Does a free VIN check show finance owing, stolen status or write-offs?

No. A free VIN check only reads the code itself. Finance, write-off and stolen information sits in official records. The PPSR search reports recorded security interests and available vehicle indicators from linked records. Stolen-vehicle information is not available through PPSR for Tasmanian vehicles, so follow the relevant Tasmanian Government check as well.

What is a VIN number?

A VIN (vehicle identification number) is a unique 17-character code assigned to a vehicle when it is built. It identifies the maker, the vehicle details, the model year, the plant and the production serial number.

Which letters can a VIN not contain?

The letters I, O and Q are never used, because they are easily confused with the numbers 1 and 0. If you find one in a VIN you have copied, you have probably misread a character.

Why does my VIN fail the check digit?

The check digit in position 9 is only mandatory for North American vehicles. Many Australian, Japanese and European vehicles do not use it, so a genuine VIN can fail. Treat a mismatch as a prompt to compare the VIN with the car and paperwork, not as proof of a problem.

How many characters does a VIN have?

Vehicles supplied to the Australian market from 1 January 1989 must carry a 17-character VIN under Australian Design Rule 61. Older vehicles can have shorter or non-standard chassis numbers.

Where do I find the VIN on my car?

Common places are the base of the windscreen on the driver side, the compliance plate in the engine bay or door pillar, the firewall, and the registration and insurance papers. See our guide on where to find the VIN number.

Can I check a VIN by number plate?

This tool needs the VIN. The PPSR search also needs a VIN or chassis number and cannot be run from a number plate or engine number. Your state road authority can help you find the VIN linked to a plate you own.

What is the difference between a VIN check and a PPSR check?

A VIN check reads and validates the code. A PPSR check searches the government register using the VIN to show whether money is owing on the vehicle and whether it is recorded as written off or stolen.

Is my VIN stored or shared?

This site does not store VINs. Format checks run in your browser as you type. If you press Check & Decode VIN, the VIN is sent to the NHTSA vPIC public database to look up vehicle details, and NHTSA handles it under its own policies.

Why did the decode return little or no vehicle detail?

The decode uses NHTSA vPIC, a United States database. It does not hold every vehicle sold in Australia, so Australian-market models, older vehicles and some imports can return limited data. A missing decode does not mean the VIN is invalid.

Do the last three characters of a VIN have to be numbers?

Australian Government guidance says the last three characters of a VIN must be numbers. Some older or foreign-market vehicles may not follow that rule, so if yours does not, compare the VIN with the vehicle and its papers rather than assuming a problem.
